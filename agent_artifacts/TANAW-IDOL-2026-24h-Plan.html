01Scope decision — read before anything

This concept is not Challenge 4, and claiming it is will hurt you

You said you were leaning Smarter Field Observation. Taken honestly, a bill scanner is not field observation — Challenge 4's own wording is about utility vehicles, dashcams, smartphones and low-cost environmental sensors capturing environmental and infrastructure data. A photo of a piece of paper is document capture. Judges wrote that challenge; they will see the stretch immediately, and a visible stretch costs more than a narrower claim.

Submit under Challenge 2, with Challenge 1 as a genuine extension of the same pipeline. That is an honest two-challenge answer rather than a strained three-challenge one, and it is exactly what your constraint — one problem, properly solved — asks for.

There is one way to earn Challenge 4 legitimately: photographing the meter, not the bill. That is a field asset capturing infrastructure data. Seven-segment digital meters OCR at 99%+ with low-confidence rejection in published work. But analog dial meters should not be attempted — the best published whole-meter accuracy starts at 75% and only reaches 98.9% with a custom angle-regression model. Many Philippine legacy meters are dials. My advice: skip it. It is a second capture pipeline, a second failure mode, and it buys a challenge tag you do not need.

The one thing to verify right now

Get a real Meralco bill in front of you and confirm four things. The whole build rests on them:

  1. The "Monthly Consumption Tracker" — research says it shows up to 24 months of monthly kWh as a bar graph with the numeric values printed as text beneath the bars. Confirm the numbers are really there as text, and count how many months carry a number. Meralco's wording is "up to," newer accounts show fewer, so your parser must handle a variable count no matter what you see.
  2. The temperature row. The bill reportedly prints each month's average temperature alongside consumption, compared to the same month last year. Confirm it. If it is there, you may not even need an external weather API for the demo.
  3. The subsidies line. "Subsidies – Lifeline and Senior Citizen Discounts" reportedly shares one printed line with the Tax Recovery Adjustment Charge — two values, one label. Classic parser trap.
  4. Which month's rates the bill uses. TANAW looks up Meralco's published rates by month, so you must know which month's schedule a bill was charged at. Test it: divide this bill's Generation Charge by its kWh and find the matching month in Meralco's tables. Also confirm the "Previous Reading" and "Current Reading" fields — you will use them to check the OCR.

Source caution: the field-level detail above came from a third-party mirror of Meralco's help center, not from meralco.com.ph, which was not reachable during research. It corroborates independently and reads as verbatim, but one real bill settles it in two minutes.

02The idea

The single problem: a Meralco customer cannot tell why their bill changed. Was it them, was it the weather, or was it the rate? Without that answer, no decision they make about their electricity is an informed one.

TANAW: photograph the paper bill — no login, no app, no account. Recover two years of monthly consumption and every charge line from that one photo, and answer the question in a sentence a person can act on, in seconds. Meralco's own published rate schedules supply last month's rates, so the rate change splits line by line from that one photo.

Everything else is the same model, read differently. That is what makes this one problem and not three:

Why it changed

One photo: “Your bill rose ₱840. ₱520 was a hotter month. ₱190 was the generation charge. ₱70 was other charges. ₱60 was your usage.” Every rate number from Meralco's own published schedules. The core output, and the thing nobody currently provides.

What's coming

The same fitted model, run forward on a temperature forecast. Not a new feature — the model already contains it.

Whether solar pays

The same 24-month series, priced. Your consumption shape is precisely the input a solar calculation needs, and no Philippine tool currently uses it.

What the explanation covers — and what it cannot

The common causes of bill shock in the Philippines fall into four groups. A bill can show some of them exactly, estimate one, and cannot show the rest. Being precise about which is which is part of the product.

The calculation — about 30 lines, every peso traceable

# usage effect: how much more or less you used, at last month's prices
usage_effect   = (kwh_now - kwh_prev) * rate_prev_total
weather_part   = slope * (cdd_now - cdd_prev)          # from the regression, in kWh
weather_pesos  = weather_part * rate_prev_total
you_pesos      = usage_effect - weather_pesos

# rate effect: what each charge line did to this month's kWh
for line in charge_lines:                              # generation, transmission, ...
    rate_effect[line] = (rate_now[line] - rate_prev[line]) * kwh_now

# check: usage_effect + sum(rate_effect) == bill_now - bill_prev  (± fixed charges)

Fixed monthly charges (supply, metering) do not scale with kWh — handle them as their own line and the total reconciles. rate_prev comes from Meralco's rate schedule for last month, at the row closest to the customer's kWh.

Where last month's rates come from: Meralco's own schedules

Meralco's Rates & Charges page links three PDFs per month for January–October 2026. The one to use is "Residential Bills at Typical Consumption Levels": the per-kWh rate of every charge — generation, transmission, system loss, distribution, supply, metering, universal charges, subsidies, taxes — at consumption levels from 50 to 5,000 kWh, with separate lifeline rows. The numbers are readable text. September's 200 kWh row totals ₱14.7424/kWh, matching the independently confirmed September rate.

That gives you last month's rates for every charge line, at the customer's own consumption level, from Meralco's own document — no second photo, no hand-typed guesses.

Build it once, offline — about 2–3 hours, one person

  1. Download the ten "Typical Consumption Levels" PDFs. The URLs follow three different patterns and the newest months use random file names, so a scraper cannot predict them — open each month's three links and pick the right one.
  2. Extract the tables with pdfplumber. Column headers come out jumbled, so map each column to its charge by position, once.
  3. Validate: each row's charges must sum to its total, and September at 200 kWh must equal ₱14.7424.
  4. Save rates.json: month → consumption level → charge → ₱/kWh. Commit it.
  5. Write reasons.json: one sentence per month, with date and link, from Meralco's own rate announcements. Only where Meralco stated a reason — the rates page itself gives only one example (April: +₱0.53/kWh generation, blamed on the peso weakening against the dollar).

Do not scrape live during the demo. No API, unpredictable URLs, venue wifi. In the pitch, the production version is a monthly update job — or Meralco publishing the same table as a data feed. Check the site's robots.txt and terms before downloading; ten public documents fetched once is low-risk.

Three small differences to label on screen

  • The tables assume a 0.50% local franchise tax; the real rate varies by city.
  • Use the lifeline rows for customers at 100 kWh or below.
  • From May 2026 an AWAT refund of ₱0.2511/kWh appears as its own line, and October's rate includes temporary refunds — keep refund lines separate so they do not hide the underlying change.

The bill checks its own OCR

With no login, OCR is on the critical path — and a misread number produces a confidently wrong explanation. The bill contains enough of its own arithmetic to catch most errors:

  1. Current Reading − Previous Reading = this month's kWh (allow for a meter multiplier if one is printed).
  2. The latest bar in the tracker = this month's kWh.
  3. The charge lines sum to "Charges for this billing period."
  4. Remaining Balance + Charges for this billing period + Installment Due = Total Amount Due.

If every check passes, show the explanation instantly. If one fails, highlight the suspect field on a confirm-and-correct screen and let the customer fix one number. This turns "OCR is unreliable" into a feature: a judge will be impressed that the app knows when it has misread the bill.

The framing that makes this land with Meralco

The bill already prints the kWh and the temperature side by side — and leaves the customer to do the arithmetic in their head. You are not proposing new data collection. You are doing the calculation Meralco's own bill already implies.

Two supporting facts worth having ready. Meralco's own Appliance Calculator instructs customers to read the 12-month average off their bill by hand and multiply it by a rate — you automate the exact step they tell people to do manually. And "bill shock" is Meralco's and the ERC's own term for this problem, not yours.

Do not say Meralco gives customers no consumption history — the printed bill plainly has two years of it, and someone in that room designed it. The defensible claim is narrower and stronger: Meralco surfaces bills, not explanation.

03The method

This is the part that separates you from an app. The technique is a published standard, not something invented tonight.

Variable-base degree-day change-point regression

Known as the 3PC model in ASHRAE Guideline 14, and as the billing-period method in CalTRACK, which US utilities use to settle efficiency payments in money. Originally the PRISM model, Fels 1986. It fits a household's monthly kWh against cooling degree-days, searching for the balance-point temperature at which that home starts cooling, and splits consumption into baseload and weather-driven components.

It needs only what you already have: monthly kWh, billing period dates, and temperature. No smart meter. No interval data. No training set. No survey. Roughly six hours of numpy, pandas and scipy.

The algorithm, as CalTRACK §3 specifies it

  • Grid-search candidate balance points, requiring a minimum number of degree-days per candidate
  • Days-weighted least squares — billing periods are not equal length
  • Screen out non-negative coefficient violations
  • Highest adjusted R² wins
  • Apply a CV(RMSE) screen and refuse to report when the fit fails it

Numbers for the slide

Validated against submetered end-use data: baseload split error −5.0%, total-load adjusted R² mean 0.93. An independent 2022 study comparing change-point regression against submetered heating and cooling found r > 0.95 with no significant difference by ANOVA. Calendarizing billing periods before fitting gave a ~19% average R² gain across 50,345 buildings — cheap, large, do it.

The real technical risk — design around it from the first line of code

Every published accuracy figure comes from temperate climates with 30–50 °C annual swings. Metro Manila's monthly means span roughly 3–5 °C, so the balance point is weakly identified. This is the thing that could make your output meaningless. Three mitigations:

  1. Compute cooling degree-days from daily temperature, then aggregate to the billing period. Never compute them from monthly means. This is what the standard actually prescribes, and it is the difference between the method working and not.
  2. Use all 24 periods if the bill provides them.
  3. Refuse to display a decomposition below R² = 0.7, falling back to a fixed-base 24 °C two-parameter model and saying so on screen. PRISM used that cutoff in 1986.

A tool that knows when to stay silent reads as rigorous. One that always produces a number reads as a toy. Build the refusal path and demo it — show a household where the fit fails and the app declines to guess. That single screen will do more for your credibility than any feature.

One design decision the evidence makes for you

A field experiment at PLN in Indonesia found that economic and appliance-cost information cut consumption 3.15% — but social comparison increased it 3.10%, a boomerang effect. Its explicit recommendation was to put informational nudges on the monthly bill.

So: do not build a "compare yourself to your neighbours" feature. It is the obvious thing to add, it is what everyone else will add, and the closest regional evidence says it backfires. Saying this out loud in Q&A is a strong moment.

Why not NILM — and why saying so helps you

Appliance-level disaggregation is the glamorous answer and it is ruled out on published grounds: the literature's floor is 15–60 minute data, the sweet spot is 1 Hz, degradation as sampling rate falls is nonlinear, and cross-dataset transfer loses 40–50%. Meralco's AMI customers get daily totals by SMS — below anything in the literature. Knowing why you did not do the obvious thing demonstrates command of the field.

04The solar extension

Two hours of work, and it carries the Clean Energy challenge on the back of the same data. It also contains the single most persuasive screen in your demo.

The finding the whole extension rests on

Under Philippine net metering, exported energy is credited at the monthly blended generation cost, not the retail rate — confirmed from Meralco's own FAQ. Credits carry forward and are never paid in cash. Cap is 100 kW.

Against a March 2026 generation charge of ₱7.86/kWh and an October 2026 retail rate of ₱15.06/kWh, an exported kWh is worth about half a self-consumed one.

Of the Philippine solar tools inspected — including the DOE's own sizing guidance and MSpectrum, Meralco's own solar subsidiary — none discloses or uses an export credit rate, and none separates self-consumption from export. Every one therefore overstates savings, in the same direction. MSpectrum has no calculator at all; its inquiry form asks for your address and account number, not your kWh.

That is nearly your pitch, peer-reviewed. A 2020 study bounds the honest limit: monthly-average sizing carries ≥40% uncertainty, falling to 2.2% with sub-30-minute data. Claim the energy and seasonality baseline; disclose the intraday limit. Lead the interface with payback years rather than peso savings — published work finds payback stable under load-shape uncertainty while savings swing 10–15%.

Your hardest comparison, and how to beat it

The 2023 champion, Project One, was a consumption-tracking and bill-projection app — Challenge 2 is the most crowded track in this hackathon's history. Do not pitch tracking. Tracking shows you a number you already have. Your difference is attribution: a published econometric method that says how much of the change was weather, how much was rate, and how much was behaviour — and that refuses to answer when the data will not support it. Say the word “explanation” and avoid the word “tracker.”

13Q&A drill

  1. How is this different from the 2023 winner, or from any consumption tracker? → Tracking shows a number you already have. This attributes the change — weather, rate, behaviour — using a published standard, and refuses when the data cannot support it. Rehearse this most.
  2. Does this actually work in a tropical climate? → Be straight: every published validation is temperate, Manila's seasonal swing is small, and that weakens balance-point identification. So we compute degree-days from daily temperature, use all 24 periods, and gate on R². Show the refusal screen as the answer.
  3. Where do your rate numbers come from? → Meralco's own monthly "Residential Bills at Typical Consumption Levels" schedules, at the row matching the customer's consumption. Every peso traces to a Meralco document.
  4. What if the OCR misreads the bill? → The bill checks itself: readings, tracker, line sum and total must agree. If any fails, the app asks the customer to confirm one number instead of guessing.
  5. Why not just put this in the My Meralco app? → It could live there too. But a photo reaches customers who never get past the login, and it needs no integration to start.
  6. Can it tell me the generation charge rose because of fuel prices or the peso? → No, and say so. Those sit inside the generation charge; the bill shows that it moved, not why. Where Meralco has stated a reason, TANAW quotes it with the date.
  7. Why not appliance-level disaggregation? → The literature's floor is 15–60 minute data; your AMI customers get daily totals by SMS. NILM below that resolution is not supported by any published result.
  8. We already have the data — why do we need a photo? → You do not. The photo is how it works for a customer today with no integration. Behind your billing system it runs for 8.3 million people with no new metering.
  9. How accurate is the split? → Published validation against submetered data: baseload error −5.0%, adjusted R² around 0.93. Then volunteer the tropical caveat before they press.
  10. Where did the solar numbers come from? → Cost from an independent 2026 analysis, yield from the DOE's own published figure, and the rates from the customer's bill. The self-consumption share is an explicit assumption because no Philippine daytime-load data exists.
  11. Is your export-credit claim right? → It is from Meralco's own net metering FAQ: exports are valued at the blended generation cost, not retail, and credits are never paid in cash. One secondary source says otherwise and is wrong.
  12. Why not add neighbour comparison? → Deliberately excluded. The nearest regional evidence, a field experiment at PLN in Indonesia, found social comparison increased consumption 3.1% while economic information cut it 3.15%.
  13. What would you need from us? → Billing data for a sample of households, and the actual bill template so the parser is robust. Then a pilot measuring whether explanation changes behaviour.
  14. Why no chatbot? → Deliberately left out. Every peso TANAW shows comes from the bill or Meralco's published schedules, and a chatbot can invent a reason that sounds right. Meralco already has an AI assistant in MIDAS; TANAW's job is a precise, traceable explanation, not conversation.
  15. Privacy? → The bill carries an account number. Parse locally, store nothing identifying, and say so. Have the answer even if it is not built.
